## Topic 1: Why India's Market Regulator Needed an AI to Catch the Fraud It Was Already Watching For

- **Category:** FINANCE / BUSINESS
- **Pattern:** India news/scandal explained (#4), framed with a "why does
  this happen" hook (#1) — SEBI's own AI build-out as the story, not a single
  bad actor.
- **The hook:** SEBI's surveillance desk now runs AI tools across trading
  data and social media, sends out roughly 7,000 takedown requests a month —
  about 40% more than pandemic-era levels — and an AI-generated alert helped
  trigger a real enforcement action against a JPMorgan Chase unit within six
  days. A regulator that already had subpoena power and a market-wide data
  feed still needed a bigger algorithm. What does that say about how
  fraud actually spreads now?
- **Why it's underrated / why now:** This is one-day-old news (Bloomberg,
  Sept 22, 2026), not a retrospective, and it's a rare regulatory story with
  a concrete, checkable before/after: SEBI's surveillance/engineering
  headcount has grown from ~20 to 200+ in five years, its social-media
  takedown volume has a specific number attached, and there's a named,
  dated enforcement outcome (the JPMorgan unit action) tied directly to an
  AI-flagged alert — not just "SEBI says it's using AI," but a case where
  it demonstrably worked.
- **Real anchor:** Bloomberg, "India's SEBI Taps AI to Police Traders, Fight
  Market Fraud" (published 2026-09-22), corroborated by Business Standard
  ("From trades to balance sheets, Sebi widens its AI-powered surveillance
  net" / "Sebi taps AI in growing fight against market manipulation and
  fraud"), Angel One, The420.in, and Whalesbook coverage of the same
  disclosures — specific figures: ~7,000 monthly takedown requests across X,
  Instagram and Telegram (≈40% above pandemic-era levels); surveillance/
  engineering staff up from ~20 to 200+ over five years; an AI-flagged
  anomaly cited among the factors behind SEBI's enforcement action against a
  JPMorgan Chase unit, six days after the alert. Writer should pull the

## Topic 2: Why Millions of Indians Still Won't Fill Out the One Form That Decides Who Inherits Their Shares

- **Category:** PERSONAL FINANCE / MONEY
- **Pattern:** "Why do WE [universal behavior]" (#2), crossed with a dated
  India money explainer (#3) — the SEBI rule change is the news hook, the
  psychology is the actual story.
- **The hook:** Since September 1, 2026, SEBI has required every new
  single-holder demat account and mutual fund folio to either name a
  nominee or formally opt out — and, tellingly, it didn't back the rule with
  a threat to freeze existing non-compliant accounts. Instead it mandated
  bi-annual SMS/email nudges and a first-login pop-up. SEBI's own framing is
  the giveaway: this isn't investors refusing to nominate someone, it's
  investors who mean to and never quite do it. Why does a two-minute form
  that decides who inherits your money keep losing to "later"?
- **Why it's underrated / why now:** The rule took effect this month
  (Sept 1, 2026) and is still being actively explained/clarified in the
  financial press (BusinessToday, ValueResearchOnline, Newsdrum all ran
  pieces in the days around the deadline), so it's timely without being a
  same-day breaking story that will feel stale by publish time. It also
  gives the site a genuine two-sided "full picture" angle — regulatory
  design (why nudges instead of penalties) plus reader psychology (why the
  form gets skipped) — rather than a flat "here's how to add a nominee"
  utility piece.
- **Real anchor:** SEBI's revised nomination framework for demat accounts
  and mutual fund folios, effective 1 September 2026 (reported by
  BusinessToday, ValueResearchOnline, Newsdrum, CorpLawUpdates, Sansa Legal)
  — mandatory nomination-or-opt-out for new single-holder accounts;
  depositories/RTAs required to display nomination status on statements,
  send bi-annual reminders, and show a first-login pop-up for existing
  accounts with neither a nomination nor an opt-out on file; SEBI's stated
  rationale is reducing the pool of unclaimed securities left in limbo after
  an investor's death. Pair with the real, published economics literature on
  present bias and deadline-driven procrastination (e.g. the NBER working
  paper "Present-Bias, Procrastination and Deadlines in a Field Experiment,"
  w19874) for the "why do we personally keep skipping this" layer — not a
  finance-specific study, but directly applicable, and a citation in the
  same spirit as the site's existing use of "pain of paying" / "fresh-start
  effect" research. Writer should pull SEBI's actual circular/framework
  document and verify the current unclaimed-assets figure independently
  before publishing rather than citing secondary aggregator summaries.
- **Angle source:** General trend scan (WebSearch) — BLOG_TOPICS_SOURCES.md
  §5.
