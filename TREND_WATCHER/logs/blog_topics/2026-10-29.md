## Topic 1: Why Your Bank Can Still Lock Your Phone Over an Unpaid Loan — Just Not Without 30 Days' Warning Anymore

- **Category:** PERSONAL FINANCE / FINANCE
- **Pattern:** Shape 4 (breaking India news/regulation, explained, FACT-dense
  numbered breakdown) crossed with Shape 1 (the everyday friction is a
  genuine, widely-feared one — a lender reaching into your phone over money
  you owe).
- **The hook:** For years, digital lending apps could — and did — threaten to
  or actually disable a borrower's phone, or worse, scrape its photos and
  contacts to shame people into repaying, with essentially no rulebook
  governing when or how. The RBI first cautioned against this in December
  2020, after media reports linked the practice to borrower suicides. Nearly
  six years later, the RBI has finally written the actual rules: new
  Responsible Business Conduct amendment directions (notified August 6,
  2026) say a lender can restrict a borrower's device only if that specific
  device was bought with that specific loan — never for an unrelated loan —
  and even then only after the account is 30 days overdue, with a full
  restriction possible only after 60 days and two prior notices. Emergency
  calls, SMS and incoming calls must keep working throughout. Get it wrong —
  restrict a device wrongfully, or fail to unlock it fast enough once the
  borrower pays — and the lender owes compensation of ₹250 per hour of
  wrongful restriction, capped at the loan amount.
- **Why it's underrated / why now:** This is a live regulatory response to a
  scandal most readers remember only as "those loan apps that drove people to
  suicide a few years ago" — few will have seen that the RBI's actual fix
  finally landed this year, with a real compensation formula attached. It's
  also a clean, concrete lesson in why "the bank can do whatever it wants
  once you're in default" is false — the rule exists precisely because, for
  years, it effectively wasn't.
- **Real anchor:** RBI's Reserve Bank of India (Commercial Banks / NBFCs —
  Responsible Business Conduct) Amendment Directions, 2026, notified August
  6, 2026 (RBI notification reference RBI/2026-27/230 for the NBFC-facing
  amendment), restricting device-locking to loans that financed the device
  itself, gated behind 30-day (partial) and 60-day (full) overdue thresholds
  with prior notice, mandatory availability of emergency functions, and
  ₹250/hour compensation for wrongful or delayed restoration, capped at the
  loan amount — reported consistently across vinodkothari.com, Agrud
  Partners' regulatory summary, corplawupdates.in and taxupdate.in. Effective
  date is reported as January 1, 2027. Historical anchor: RBI's December 23,
  2020 public advisory cautioning against unauthorised digital lending
  apps/platforms, issued after reports tying app-based harassment to borrower
  suicides; RBI's 2022 Digital Lending Guidelines requiring upfront
  disclosure of the partner bank/NBFC; and RBI's penalty against Bajaj
  Finance over recovery-agent harassment of customers.
  **Caveat for the writer:** secondary sources disagree on the exact
  instrument name (some call it the "Fourth Amendment," the NBFC notification
  cites "Third Amendment Directions, 2026") and on whether the October 2026
  date floated in earlier draft-stage coverage still applies to any part of
  the framework — pull the RBI's own notification text (search RBI/2026-27/230
  and the matching commercial-bank-facing circular on rbi.org.in, which
  `WebFetch` could not reach this pass) before citing the instrument name or
  effective date in the published post.
- **Angle source:** General India RBI/banking-regulation WebSearch sweep per

## Topic 2: Why Your Dinner Out Keeps Getting Pricier Even When the Menu Hasn't Changed

- **Category:** LIFESTYLE / BUSINESS
- **Pattern:** Shape 1 ("why does this everyday friction happen" consumer
  explainer) — a hidden, recurring cost pass-through most diners never see
  on the bill.
- **The hook:** The menu price looks the same as last month. The actual
  margin behind it doesn't. Commercial LPG — the fuel every restaurant,
  roadside stall, cloud kitchen and caterer in the country cooks on — got
  hiked for the second month running on October 1, 2026: a 19kg commercial
  cylinder rose ₹62.50 in Delhi (to ₹2,810), ₹71.50 in Patna (to ₹3,100.50),
  with similar rises in Mumbai and Chennai, landing squarely in the run-up to
  peak festive-season dining demand. Restaurants rarely reprint a menu over a
  ₹60-70 cylinder hike — they absorb it quietly, in thinner margins, smaller
  portions, a "service charge" that creeps up, or a price rise that shows up
  months later with no obvious trigger. The diner never connects their bill
  to a fuel-price notification they never saw.
- **Why it's underrated / why now:** It's a dated, verifiable price move
  (not a vague "eating out is getting expensive" complaint) landing at
  exactly the moment Indians eat out most — Diwali and Dhanteras are days
  away. It also names a real, specific mechanism (sticky, invisible
  cost-pass-through on recurring B2B inputs) rather than blaming inflation in
  general, which distinguishes it from the macro festive-spending pieces
  already run in this folder (2026-10-23) and from the reference-price/retail
  pricing piece run just last pass (2026-10-28) — that one was about phone
  prices getting anchored upward before a "discount"; this one is about a
  cost rise nobody prices in at all, on the other side of the counter.
- **Real anchor:** Oil marketing companies' October 1, 2026 revision of
  19kg commercial LPG cylinder rates — Delhi ₹2,810 (up ₹62.50 from ₹2,747.50
  in September), Patna ₹3,100.50 (up ₹71.50, the largest rise reported),
  with comparable increases reported in Mumbai (₹2,764) and Chennai (₹2,983)
  — reported by Business Today, Angel One, Republic World and NewsBytes,
  following a smaller ₹9.50 Delhi rise in September 2026 (confirming this is
  a second consecutive monthly hike). Domestic 14.2kg cylinder rates were
  explicitly unchanged in the same revision. **Caveat for the writer:** no
  source quantified an actual menu-price pass-through percentage this
  pass — the piece should frame the mechanism (cost rises rarely get
  reversed in menu pricing even when input costs later fall) as the
  argument, not claim a specific pass-through number that wasn't found in
  reporting.
- **Angle source:** General India price-hike WebSearch sweep per
  `BLOG_TOPICS_SOURCES.md` §5.
