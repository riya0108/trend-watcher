## Topic 1: Why Your Bond Investment Is About to Get a Traffic Light — and Why Green Doesn't Mean Safe

- **Category:** PERSONAL FINANCE
- **Pattern:** Shape 4 (breaking India regulatory news, explained — dated,
  FACT-dense, explicit sourcing) crossed with Shape 1's "why does this
  everyday friction happen" hook — the friction being that a new-looking
  safety label can create false confidence about exactly the risk it
  doesn't measure.
- **The hook:** From November 21, 2026, every bond ad, offer document and
  bond-buying app screen in India has to show a six-colour "Credit
  Risk-o-Meter" next to the investment — the same visual-gauge idea SEBI
  already forced onto mutual funds years ago, now extended to debt
  securities. It sounds like bonds just got safer to buy. SEBI's own rule
  says otherwise: the meter measures only credit/default risk and
  explicitly does not measure interest-rate risk or liquidity risk — the
  two ways a "safe" bond has actually burned retail investors in India
  before (DHFL, IL&FS, Yes Bank AT1).
- **Why it's underrated / why now:** This is 8 days out from this pass, with
  a hard, specific compliance date, and coverage so far is almost entirely
  law-firm/compliance-newsletter language (circular summaries, consultation-
  paper recaps) — nothing translating it into "what this traffic light
  actually tells you versus what it doesn't" for a reader using a bond app
  like Wint Wealth or an OBPP for the first time. The genuine two-sided
  story: SEBI is visibly responding to a real retail-bondholder trust
  problem, and the fix is real — but a green light on credit risk says
  nothing about whether you can sell the bond before maturity or what
  happens to its price if rates move, which is exactly the blind spot that
  burns first-time retail bond buyers who treat "fixed income" as a synonym
  for "safe."
- **Real anchor:** SEBI circular dated October 7, 2026, making the Credit
  Risk-o-Meter mandatory on offer documents, abridged prospectuses, private
  placement memorandums, all issuer/OBPP advertisements, for listed or
  to-be-listed NCDs, commercial papers, securitised debt instruments,
  security receipts and market-linked debentures — coming into force 45
  days from issuance (November 21, 2026 per multiple secondary sources;
  confirm the exact calendar date against SEBI's own circular text before
  publishing, since the circular itself states "45 days from issuance"
  rather than a calendar date). Reported consistently across
  corplawupdates.in, niftytrader.in, policyedge.in, moneylife.in,
  taxguru.in, caalley.com, and confirmed by a government source,
  newsonair.gov.in (All India Radio), October 8, 2026. SEBI's own stated
  limitation — "not investment advice... does not measure interest-rate
  risk, liquidity or price volatility" — is reported directly from the
  circular's language across these sources; flag this caveat explicitly in
  any draft since it's the actual point of the piece, not a footnote.
- **Angle source:** General SEBI/RBI regulatory-news WebSearch sweep
  (`BLOG_TOPICS_SOURCES.md` §5 "General trend scan").

## Topic 2: Why Reporting Your Bank Fraud Within 24 Hours Still Doesn't Get Your Money Back

- **Category:** PERSONAL FINANCE
- **Pattern:** Shape 4 — breaking India news/report, explained (FACT-dense,
  survey-based, explicit sourcing), built on a genuine systemic failure
  rather than a single villain.
- **The hook:** Banks and the RBI have spent years telling Indians the same
  thing: report a fraudulent transaction within 24 hours and you're
  protected. A nationwide survey of 8,306 citizens across 16 states says
  that promise mostly doesn't hold up in practice — 72% of digital
  financial-fraud victims who were surveyed got none of their money back,
  even though 63% of them reported the fraud to their bank inside that
  same 24-hour window. And before most victims even get to that point,
  nearly 3 in 10 who tried to file a police complaint over cybercrime say
  they had to pay a bribe to get it registered or acted on — rising to
  roughly half among poorer and rural victims, versus about 1 in 8 among
  affluent ones.
- **Why it's underrated / why now:** This report dropped roughly 5-6 weeks
  ago (late September/early October 2026) and ran almost entirely as
  UPSC-exam-prep and policy-wonk coverage, not as a consumer-facing "here's
  what actually happens to you if you get scammed" piece — exactly the gap
  this blog's house style fills. It's also a genuine complement, not a
  repeat, of the Nov 8 pass's Supreme Court/RBI digital-arrest liability
  piece: that story was about a court-ordered fix headed toward the banking
  system over the next seven weeks; this story is the ground-truth of what
  happens to a fraud complaint and a bank's "report in 24 hours" promise
  *today*, via police stations and bribes, not bank boardrooms — a
  different fact, a different mechanism, a different villain (systemic
  police/bank friction, not bank negligence).
- **Real anchor:** Status of Policing in India Report (SPIR) 2026 —
  "Cybercrime: Victim Perspectives and Systemic Responses," a joint study
  by Common Cause and Lokniti-CSDS, surveying 8,306 respondents across 16
  states, released roughly late September/early October 2026. Reported
  figures, consistent across multiple outlets: ~13% of respondents
  experienced cybercrime in the past 2-3 years; of those, 54% faced
  financial fraud and 23% lost more than ₹20,000; only ~51% formally
  complained to police (11% started but didn't follow through); 27% of
  complainants paid a bribe to get the complaint registered/acted on,
  rising to 51% among poorer victims versus 12% among affluent ones; 72%
  of digital-fraud victims recovered no money despite 63% reporting to
  their bank within 24 hours. **Caveat for the eventual draft:** these
  figures are drawn from secondary coverage (Moneylife, superkalam.com,
  civilsdaily.com, madhyamamonline.com and similar exam-prep/news
  summaries of the report), not the primary Common Cause/Lokniti-CSDS PDF
  directly — re-verify the exact percentages against the report itself (or
  Common Cause's own release) before publishing, since secondary sources
  showed minor figure discrepancies (e.g. 49% vs. 51% on the police-
  complaint rate) against each other.
- **Angle source:** General India consumer-fraud/policing-news WebSearch
  sweep (`BLOG_TOPICS_SOURCES.md` §5 "General trend scan").
