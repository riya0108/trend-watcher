## Topic 1: Why Your Insurance Agent Was Never on Your Side (And IRDAI Just Admitted It)

- **Category:** PERSONAL FINANCE / MONEY
- **Pattern:** "Why does [everyday friction] happen" (#1), dated to a
  same-week India regulatory story (#3) — the reader's own experience of
  being sold the "wrong" policy is the hook, IRDAI's own numbers are the
  proof.
- **The hook:** Nearly every Indian who's bought life, health or motor
  insurance has felt it: the agent steers you toward one policy over
  another for reasons that were never fully explained. On September 23,
  2026, IRDAI itself put a number on why — in a new consultation paper, the
  regulator proposed slashing commission caps because current payouts are
  so distorted they actively reward mis-selling: credit-life commissions of
  up to 28% (proposed cap: 2%), health commissions of up to 40% (proposed
  cap: 5%), and general/motor own-damage commissions of up to 16%
  (proposed cap: 5%), with loan-linked motor third-party commissions cut to
  nil. The regulator isn't alleging a few bad agents — it's rebuilding the
  entire fee structure because the incentives were the problem all along.
- **Why it's underrated / why now:** This is a fresh, dated regulatory
  document (released September 23, 2026, three days old at scan time) with
  a hard, checkable comment deadline (October 25, 2026) and an implementation
  horizon (FY28, from April 2027) — not a retrospective think-piece. It also
  lets the site do its "full picture" thing: pairing a number-heavy
  regulatory story with the universal reader experience of insurance
  mis-selling, which almost every reader has a personal anecdote about.
  Distinct from the site's existing "Best Lifetime Free Credit Cards"-style
  money explainers — this is about why the product you were sold looked the
  way it did, not which product to pick.
- **Real anchor:** IRDAI's public consultation paper "Recalibrating
  Economics of Insurance Distribution," released September 23, 2026 (widely
  reported by Business Standard, BusinessToday, Business Recorder, Outlook
  Money, TaxGuru, Punjab Kesari, Whalesbook, Axczel Research, Angel One).
  Specific proposed caps vs. current payouts: credit-life commission cut
  from up to 28% to 2%; health insurance commission cut from up to 40% to
  5%; motor own-damage commission cut from up to 16% to 5%; loan-packaged
  motor third-party commission cut to nil; life insurance first-year
  commission on 10-year-plus traditional policies proposed at 25% (down
  from current levels), with a simpler three-tier distribution structure
  (Insurance Distribution Entities / Insurance Distribution Persons / Market
  Infrastructure Institutions) replacing the current framework. Stakeholder
  comments are open until October 25, 2026; commission reductions are
  proposed to phase in from FY28 (April 2027). Writer should pull IRDAI's
  actual consultation paper (irdai.gov.in) directly for the full commission
  table rather than relying on secondary aggregator summaries, and should
  frame this as a *proposal* open for comment, not yet a final rule.

  §5.

## Topic 2: Why You'll Pay More for the Same Thing, Even When You Know Better

- **Category:** LIFESTYLE / PERSONAL FINANCE
- **Pattern:** "Why do WE [universal behavior]" (#2) — pure reader-psychology
  piece, same family as the site's "Why We Compare Ourselves Constantly"
  idea list entries, anchored in a real, recent, India-specific study rather
  than the site's more brand-focused "why Indian brands look cheaper" piece.
- **The hook:** You've done it — bought the pricier version of something
  when the cheaper one would have worked identically. A 2026 qualitative
  study of Indian consumers found this isn't random or purely
  status-driven: across in-depth interviews, one theme dominated every
  single conversation about why people traded up — loss aversion, and
  specifically a version of it unique to collectivist households, where the
  fear isn't "I might waste money," it's "my whole family absorbs it if
  this goes wrong." The study even found a income paradox: wealthier
  buyers showed *more* tangled reasoning about premium purchases, not less.
- **Why it's underrated / why now:** It's a genuinely fresh psychological
  mechanism the site hasn't covered yet — distinct from the existing "Why
  do Indian Brands Often Look Cheaper Than Foreign Brands" post, which is
  about brand perception and packaging, not the reader's own decision
  process. It matches the site's established pattern of citing real,
  peer-reviewed-adjacent behavioural research (pain of paying, fresh-start
  effect) rather than invented stats, and gives a genuinely two-sided
  "full picture" angle: premium-buying isn't irrational vanity, it's a
  specific, nameable risk calculation.
- **Real anchor:** Gulnaaz Kohli, "Why People Choose Expensive over Cheap:
  The Psychology of Price and Quality — A Qualitative Study of Indian
  Consumers" (SSRN, abstract ID 6291898, published April 15, 2026) — a
  phenomenological study based on in-depth interviews with 8 Indian
  consumers aged 22-55; thematic analysis identified 12 behavioural themes,
  with Loss Aversion the single dominant theme (38 occurrences across the
  interviews); the paper's own framing is that premium purchasing reflects
  "rational uncertainty management" amplified by family-level loss aversion
  in collectivist households, not simple status-seeking, and documents an
  "income paradox" where wealthier participants showed more complex
  (not simpler) reasoning about premium purchases. Writer should pull the
  SSRN paper directly (papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=6291898),
  should describe it accurately as a small-sample (n=8) qualitative study —
  not a large-scale survey — and should not overstate its generalisability
  beyond what the paper itself claims.
- **Angle source:** General trend scan (WebSearch) —
  BLOG_TOPICS_SOURCES.md §5 (found via a search for recent India consumer-
  psychology research, adjacent to the Raj Shamani "money psychology"
  framing lens in §2).
