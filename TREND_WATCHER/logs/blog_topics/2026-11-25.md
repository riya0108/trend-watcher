## Topic 1: Why RBI Just Let Banks Charge Whatever They Want to Send Someone Else's Cash Home

- **Category:** PERSONAL FINANCE
- **Pattern:** #4 — breaking India news/policy explained (dated), with a
  consumer-stakes reframe matching #1's "everyday friction" lens
- **The hook:** Every month, lakhs of people without a bank account — a
  driver, a domestic worker, a migrant labourer — walk into a bank branch,
  hand over cash, and have it wired to a relative's account via NEFT. RBI
  just issued the first consolidated rulebook for exactly this service — and
  deliberately left the fee uncapped.
- **Why it's underrated / why now:** RBI's Domestic Money Transfer
  Directions, 2026 took effect November 11, 2026 — brand new, barely covered
  outside trade press, and it touches a transaction almost every urban
  reader has either used themselves or paid someone else to use (sending
  wages home, paying a cash-only vendor via a bank counter). It's also a
  clean "the rule that was supposed to protect you quietly didn't" story.
- **Real anchor:** RBI Domestic Money Transfer Directions, 2026, effective
  November 11, 2026: walk-in customers without a bank account can remit up
  to ₹50,000 per transaction via NEFT at a bank branch; the directions set
  transaction limits and record-keeping rules but explicitly do not
  prescribe a uniform fee structure, leaving banks/non-bank entities free to
  set their own "reasonable" charges (reported by Business Today,
  2026-11-12, citing the RBI notification).
- **Angle source:** Discovery query plan — RBI/banking beat
  (`RBI new rule bank customers November 2026`).

## Topic 2: Why the First Answer Google Gives You Is the One Least Worth Trusting

- **Category:** LIFESTYLE
- **Pattern:** #1 — everyday-friction "why" explainer, built on a reframed
  global AI story (per `BLOG_TOPICS_SOURCES.md` §4's Staying Ahead
  reframe-move, since the community feed itself was unreachable this pass)
- **The hook:** You Google something, read the AI Overview box at the top,
  and stop there — even though researchers found roughly 1 in 9 of its
  "verifiable" claims don't actually hold up against the sources it cites as
  proof.
- **Why it's underrated / why now:** Nearly every reader does this
  multiple times a day without thinking about it; the underlying research
  is recent enough (forthcoming at ACM's Internet Measurement Conference,
  first reported September 2026) that it hasn't saturated Indian coverage
  yet, and it's a genuine two-sided story — the Overview boxes actually cite
  more "credible-looking" domains on average, which is exactly what makes
  the wrong 11% so easy to miss.
- **Real anchor:** Washington University in St. Louis study (forthcoming,
  ACM Internet Measurement Conference 2026; first reported by WashU's
  Source news office, September 2026): researchers ran 55,393 Google
  searches over 40 days; AI Overviews appeared on 13.7% of queries; about
  11% of verifiable claims in those Overviews were unsupported by, or
  contradicted, the sources the Overview itself cited — even though cited
  domains skewed toward higher average credibility scores than ordinary
  search results.
