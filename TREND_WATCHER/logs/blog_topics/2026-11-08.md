## Topic 1: Why Your Bank May Soon Have to Pay You Back for Falling for a "Digital Arrest" Call

- **Category:** PERSONAL FINANCE
- **Pattern:** Shape 4 (breaking India news/scandal, explained — FACT-dense,
  explicit sourcing) with a genuine two-sided dispute (bank liability vs.
  compliance burden) rather than a settled villain.
- **The hook:** For years, "digital arrest" scams — fraudsters posing as
  police or customs officers on a video call, telling victims they're
  under investigation and must transfer money immediately to avoid
  arrest — have drained Indian bank accounts with the bank itself treated
  as a neutral pipe, not a party at fault. On October 29, 2026, the
  Supreme Court said that's no longer good enough: in a suo motu hearing
  led by Chief Justice Surya Kant, the Court said banks should compensate
  victims — by at least the amount lost — in cases where the bank's own
  due diligence failed to flag the fraudulent transfer, and directed the
  RBI to build that liability into a new KYC framework.
- **Why it's underrated / why now:** This is nine days old relative to this
  pass, with two hard dates still ahead of the reader: the Attorney General
  told the Court the RBI's new KYC Amendment Directions, 2026 are due to be
  notified by November 30, 2026 and fully operational by December 31,
  2026 — i.e., inside the next seven weeks. Coverage so far is almost
  entirely court-reporting (the order, dryly) or UPSC/exam-prep summaries;
  nobody's written the plain-English "your bank might now owe you your
  money back, here's the test for when, and here's the clock on when this
  actually becomes real" piece for an ordinary reader who has a parent or
  relative worried about exactly this scam. The reported ₹800+ crore lost
  to digital-arrest-style fraud in 2026 alone (per the Chief Justice's own
  remarks) gives it real scale.
- **Real anchor:** Supreme Court of India, suo motu proceeding on digital
  arrest fraud, hearing of October 29, 2026, bench led by CJI Surya Kant.
  The Court directed that RBI's proposed regulatory measures require
  financial institutions to indemnify digital-arrest/cyber-fraud victims
  with compensation "not less than the amount lost" where due diligence
  failed, and asked RBI to place the draft KYC Amendment Directions, 2026
  (addressing mule-account detection and victim compensation) before it at
  the next hearing, before November 30, 2026. Corroborated across Outlook
  Money, BFSI (ETELETS Online), and multiple current-affairs/exam-prep
  summaries (Vajiram & Ravi, iASPoint). **Caveats for the writer:** (1) the
  exact bench composition is reported inconsistently across sources (one
  names Justices Joymalya Bagchi and V. Mohana alongside CJI Surya Kant,
  another gives an incomplete third name) — verify against the Court's own
  cause-list/order before naming judges in the draft. (2) This is a
  directive from the hearing, not yet a notified RBI rule — write it as
  "the Court has told RBI to do this, by this date" rather than "RBI has
  done this." (3) An earlier order in the same matter (reported as August
  4, 2026, directing an SOP for mule accounts) comes from a single,
  less-authoritative source — treat as background context, not a citable
  fact on its own. (4) The ₹800 crore 2026 loss figure is attributed to
  the Chief Justice's remarks in court, not an independently published
  RBI/NCRB dataset — cite it as a quoted figure from the hearing, not as a
  freestanding statistic.

## Topic 2: Why That Instagram Shop You Run Almost Just Signed Away Its Content to Train Meta's AI

- **Category:** BUSINESS
- **Pattern:** Shape 1 ("why does this everyday friction happen" consumer
  explainer), built on a global AI-industry story reframed for an Indian
  small-business/creator audience — the Staying Ahead "Daily AI Updates"
  stand-in source for this pass.
- **The hook:** If you run a shop, page or ad account on Facebook,
  Instagram, WhatsApp or Messenger "for business," you've already agreed
  to Meta's Commercial Terms — the fine print almost nobody reads. Meta
  had planned to update those terms, effective November 20, 2026, in a way
  legal experts said could let it treat a business's agreement to the
  Commercial Terms as consent to scrape content tied to that business —
  including, potentially, linked websites — to train its AI. After court
  injunctions in Norway and Brazil and a UK competition-regulator probe,
  Meta quietly pulled the preview of the new terms on November 5, 2026 and
  indefinitely paused the rollout — but gave no new date, and said nothing
  about how a business could actually opt out if the terms return.
- **Why it's underrated / why now:** This is three days old relative to
  this pass. Coverage so far has been almost entirely US/EU-trade-press
  framing (publishers, journalists, European regulators) — nobody has
  written the "if you're one of the millions of Indian small businesses or
  creators running a shop or page through Meta's business tools, this was
  about you too, and it's only paused, not gone" version. It's a clean,
  concrete case of "the terms you clicked 'I agree' on can change what
  happens to your content" — a friction almost every small business owner
  with an Instagram page has experienced without ever reading the clause
  that made it possible.
- **Real anchor:** Meta paused updates to its Commercial Terms, Product
  Catalog Terms, Self-Serve Ad Terms and Ad Creative Generative AI Terms —
  which were due to take effect November 20, 2026 — removing the preview
  link and confirming the indefinite delay via its Business Help Center
  update on November 5, 2026 (reported by Reuters and Nieman Lab on
  November 6). The Commercial Terms apply specifically to business/
  commercial use of Facebook, Instagram, WhatsApp and Messenger. Legal
  experts quoted by Nieman Lab said the wording could have given Meta an
  argument that businesses had consented to scraping of content and
  linked sites tied to their business accounts, for AI training folded
  into advertising and business products. Corroborated across 404 Media,
  FourWeekMBA, Nieman Lab and AI Weekly's alert digest. **Caveats for the
  writer:** (1) no source found this pass confirms an India-specific
  statement, regulatory response, or number of affected Indian business
  accounts — write the reframe as "this is what the terms cover and who
  they apply to," not as a claim that Indian businesses or regulators have
  specifically reacted. (2) Reports attribute the pause to injunctions in
  Norway and Brazil plus a UK CMA probe — one of the three sourcing this
  detail flagged it as less firmly corroborated than the pause itself;
  verify against Meta's own Business Help Center notice before stating the
  causal link as settled fact. (3) Meta's position, per its own statement,
  is that the update "only applies to its ads and business products" and
  that its use of web crawlers generally isn't changing — present that as
  Meta's stated position alongside the critics' reading, not as resolved.
