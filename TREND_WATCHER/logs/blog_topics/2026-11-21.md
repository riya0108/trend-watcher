## Topic 1: Why SEBI Is Still Punishing People Over a Mutual Fund Scandal From 2022 — and What Front-Running Actually Cost Your SIP

- **Category:** FINANCE
- **Pattern:** Shape 4 ("breaking India news/scandal, explained") — a FACT-
  dense regulatory story with an explicit "what does this mean for me"
  payoff, same family as the SEBI/trading-guru and JPSC-JSSC posts.
- **The hook:** If you ever had money in an Axis Mutual Fund scheme between
  2021 and 2022, people were trading ahead of your fund's own orders using
  tips from inside the fund house — and SEBI is still handing out
  punishments for it, four years later. The newest order just landed this
  month.
- **Why it's underrated / why now:** SEBI's adjudicating order against
  Woodstock Broking Pvt Ltd and Marfatia Stock Broking Pvt Ltd (plus their
  two directors) is dated 3 November 2026 — a fresh ₹40 lakh combined
  penalty in the long-running Axis Mutual Fund front-running case. It's a
  live, still-unfolding saga: SEBI's July 2026 final order barred former
  Axis MF chief dealer Viresh Joshi for seven years and fined him ₹3
  crore, and this November order goes after the broking firms whose
  trading terminals were used (via remote-access software) to execute the
  front-running trades. Most readers who had a SIP running through that
  period never got a plain-language explanation of what "front-running"
  actually did to their money — and the honest answer (modest NAV drag,
  reputational damage to the fund, not a direct theft from your account)
  is more interesting and more useful than the scandal headline alone.
- **Real anchor:** SEBI adjudication order dated 3 November 2026 imposing
  ₹10 lakh penalties each on Woodstock Broking Pvt Ltd, its director
  Suresh Kanmal Jajoo, Marfatia Stock Broking Pvt Ltd, and its director
  Nishil Surendra Marfatia, for facilitating front-running of Axis Mutual
  Fund trades (April 2021–March 2022) via an individual (Prijesh Kurani)
  operating their ODIN trading terminals remotely. Reported by Moneylife,
  5paisa, LiveLawBiz and BQ Prime. Cross-checked against SEBI's July 2026
  final order barring Viresh Joshi (reported by BusinessToday, CA Alley) —
  same underlying case, different defendants/order date, so this is a new
  development, not a repeat. The piece would stick to confirmed, sourced
  claims (trust/reputational cost, modest fund-level NAV impact per
  Business Standard's expert commentary) and explicitly avoid the
  unverified rupee-skimmed figures found only on low-quality aggregators.
- **Angle source:** General India regulatory/news scan (BLOG_TOPICS_SOURCES.md
  §5). No Axis MF front-running post found in the last 3 weeks of this log

## Topic 2: Why the "Free Money" Trick Your Favourite Finance Instagrammer Swears By May Not Even Work on You

- **Category:** LIFESTYLE
- **Pattern:** Shape 2 ("why do WE [universal behaviour]") — turns the lens
  on the reader's own psychology, same family as "Why We Compare Ourselves
  Constantly."
- **The hook:** Every finance-content creator has told you some version of
  the same trick: treat a cashback or a discount as "bonus money" you can
  blow guilt-free, because your brain files windfalls and earned income
  into separate mental buckets. It's one of the most-cited findings in
  pop behavioural economics. The largest-ever test of it, just published,
  found the effect is measurably weaker the lower a country's income
  level — and India was one of the 21 countries tested.
- **Why it's underrated / why now:** "The Robustness of Mental Accounting
  Across 21 Countries" (Journal of Consumer Research, advance access 10
  July 2026) ran 5,589 participants through classic mental-accounting
  scenarios and got picked up widely only this quarter — Cambridge Judge
  Business School published its own write-up on 30 October 2026, and
  Columbia Public Health/phys.org covered it in mid-November 2026, which
  is what surfaced it this pass. The finding that's genuinely new to most
  readers: the bias isn't a fixed law of human psychology — it's
  measurably weaker in lower-income countries, and the authors themselves
  caution against assuming wealth alone explains why. For readers fed a
  constant diet of money-psychology advice assumed to apply universally,
  that's a real, checkable reason to question whether a given "money
  mistake" warning was ever describing them in the first place — not just
  Americans in a lab.
- **Real anchor:** Priolo et al., "The Robustness of Mental Accounting
  Across 21 Countries," Journal of Consumer Research (DOI
  10.1093/jcr/ucag022), advance access 10 July 2026; n=5,589 across 21
  countries including India; 90.5% of individually tested effects (133/147)
  replicated in the predicted direction, with effect sizes weaker in
  lower-income countries. Covered by Cambridge Judge Business School (30
  Oct 2026), Columbia Public Health, and phys.org (mid-Nov 2026). The post
  would report only the study's own stated finding (income-level
  association, explicitly not claimed as causal by the authors) and would
  not invent an India-specific effect size, since the coverage found
  doesn't break results out by individual country.
- **Angle source:** General trend/research scan (BLOG_TOPICS_SOURCES.md
  §5); distinct from the 2026-11-09 "₹600 beer at a 5-star hotel" post,
  which used a single classic mental-accounting demo (context-dependent
  willingness to pay) — this pitch is about the new cross-country/income
  finding on how strongly the bias holds at all, not a repeat of that
  demonstration.
