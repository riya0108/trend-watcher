## Topic 1: Why Your Car Insurance Has Always Bundled In Things You Never Picked — and Why That Changes on December 15

- **Category:** PERSONAL FINANCE
- **Pattern:** Shape 3 — dated India money explainer, crossed with Shape 1's
  "why does this everyday friction happen" engine (every car/two-wheeler
  owner has paid for this bundle without ever being asked).
- **The hook:** Your car or bike insurance premium has always quietly
  bundled together third-party liability, personal accident cover for
  yourself and your passengers, and own-damage cover into one take-it-or-
  leave-it number. From December 15, 2026, IRDAI is forcing insurers to
  break that bundle apart and let you pick what you actually want — which
  also means the regulator is implicitly admitting the old bundled design
  was never really optional for the customer.
- **Why it's underrated / why now:** IRDAI issued the circular on
  November 2, 2026, it applies to every car and two-wheeler motor policy
  issued from December 15, 2026, and it's had only trade-press coverage so
  far — no plain-language "what this means for your renewal" piece yet.
  It's also a genuinely two-sided story: third-party liability stays
  compulsory by law, but occupant/pillion personal-accident cover and
  own-damage cover become separable choices, which is real consumer
  flexibility and also a quiet acknowledgment that insurers were charging
  for things many policyholders already had covered elsewhere (e.g. a
  separate personal accident policy) and never had the option to drop.
- **Real anchor:** IRDAI circular dated November 2, 2026, mandating a
  four-layer motor insurance structure (compulsory third-party liability;
  optional owner/pillion personal accident cover; optional own-damage
  cover; optional add-ons) for private cars and two-wheelers, effective for
  policies issued from December 15, 2026; requires a mandatory "customer
  option form" at the point of sale and integration with the IRDAI's Bima
  Sugam platform. Reported by Business Today (2026-11-05), Goodreturns,
  Angel One, Telangana Today, Moneylife and NewKerala (2026-11-18).

## Topic 2: Why We Judge Someone Else's Choice Without Ever Asking How Many Options They Actually Had

- **Category:** LIFESTYLE
- **Pattern:** Shape 2 — "why do WE [universal behaviour]?" — turns the
  lens on the reader's own psychology, matching the style guide's "Why We
  Compare Ourselves Constantly" family.
- **The hook:** Someone tells you they picked a restaurant, a job offer, or
  a partner from a shortlist — and you quietly judge how much they must
  have liked it, based only on what they chose. A new study finds you're
  almost certainly ignoring the one thing that should change that judgment
  most: how many options they actually had to choose from in the first
  place.
- **Why it's underrated / why now:** This is a clean, non-overlapping "why
  do WE" piece with a real, named bias and a genuinely surprising twist —
  people correctly factor in option-set size when predicting their *own*
  future satisfaction, but systematically forget to apply the same logic
  when judging other people's choices, which quietly distorts everything
  from assuming a friend "must really love" their one dating-app match to
  dismissing someone's "settling" for a job they picked from a field of two.
  No comparison/preference-judgment piece has run in the logs checked
  (2026-09-12 through 2026-11-26); distinct from the 2026-11-24 Topic 2
  (which was about *searchers* feeling more committed to the *last* option
  in a sequential search — "perceived effort payoff") because this study is
  about *observers* misjudging *someone else's* satisfaction with zero
  regard for set size, a different mechanism and a different paper.
- **Real anchor:** Hu, B., Moon, A. & VanEpps, E., "Choice Set Size Neglect
  in Predicting Others' Preferences," *Psychological Science*, published
  7 January 2026 (Hu: University of Chicago Booth; Moon: Georgetown
  McDonough; VanEpps: Vanderbilt Owen). Across six preregistered
  experiments with 10,092 U.S. adults, observers consistently
  underestimated how much the size of a choice set affects how much a
  person likes their chosen option — correctly weighting set size when
  predicting their own satisfaction, but neglecting it when predicting
  others'. The bias weakened when attention was explicitly drawn to set
  size (e.g. being asked to recall it first, or seeing multiple set sizes
  side by side). Covered by Vanderbilt Business's own research office
  (24 June 2026) and Chicago Booth Review.
- **Angle source:** General psychology/consumer-behaviour scan, standing in
  for the Raj Shamani / Vaibhav Sisinty framing lens this pass, since
  neither surfaced a fresh dated November post (see dropped list).
