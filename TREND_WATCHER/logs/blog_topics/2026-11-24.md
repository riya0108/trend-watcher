## Topic 1: Your Detergent Got a Tax Cut Last Year. Your Bill Didn't Notice.

- **Category:** PERSONAL FINANCE (also fits BUSINESS as a pricing-mechanism piece)
- **Pattern:** Shape 1 — "why does this everyday friction happen" consumer
  explainer, with a dated regulatory-vs-market-reality angle (the GST cut
  promised relief; the receipt tells a different story).
- **The hook:** In September 2025, the government moved detergents, soaps
  and noodles from the 18% GST slab down to 5%, explicitly to cut household
  bills. Fourteen months on, HUL has raised MRPs on Surf Excel, Rin and
  Wheel by anywhere from roughly 2% to 20% depending on the pack (channel
  checks reported 10–12 November 2026), and Dabur has flagged its own
  "calibrated" increases this quarter. The tax relief is real and still in
  effect. So why does the shelf price keep climbing anyway?
- **Why it's underrated / why now:** Every GST-cut story ran in September
  2025 promised cheaper noodles and detergent; nobody followed up fourteen
  months later to check whether that promise survived contact with input-
  cost inflation. This pass found the follow-up: an Outlook Business piece
  ("From Detergents to Noodles: Why Your Grocery Bill May Go Up Despite GST
  Cuts") paired with fresh November 2026 channel-check reporting on actual
  HUL MRP hikes gives a genuine, dated, two-sided mechanism — the
  government's relief and the manufacturer's input-cost pass-through are
  both real and both true at once, which is exactly the "full picture"
  framing this blog's tagline promises. Distinct from the 2026-11-15 post
  (which was about shrinking pack sizes at a flat price) and the
  2026-11-23 post (milk's seasonal pricing rule) — this is specifically
  about a tax cut being quietly absorbed by MRP increases, a different
  mechanism from both.
- **Real anchor:** GST Council's September 2025 rate rationalisation moved
  detergents and noodles from 18% to 5% GST, effective 22 September 2025
  (Business Standard, GST Whitepaper 2025). Against that: HUL MRP increases
  reported via NDTV Profit channel checks and corroborated by NewsBytes,
  Angel One and BW Retail World (10–12 November 2026) — e.g. Surf Excel
  Quick Wash 1kg ₹235→₹245, Rin Ala 500ml ₹90→₹95, Wheel 500g ₹46→₹47;
  company framing (per reporting) cites input/packaging-cost pressure, not
  tax strategy — the post will state plainly that HUL has not confirmed or
  explained the hikes as GST-related, and will present the juxtaposition as
  the reader's own puzzle to notice, not an accusation of deliberate
  claw-back. Dabur separately flagged ~2% calibrated price increases this
  quarter (reported in the same FMCG pricing-cycle coverage).

## Topic 2: Why the Last Option You Compare Before Buying Feels Like the Right One

- **Category:** LIFESTYLE
- **Pattern:** Shape 2 — "why do WE [universal behaviour]?" — turns the lens
  on the reader's own psychology, matching the style guide's "Why We
  Compare Ourselves Constantly" vein.
- **The hook:** You scroll through fifteen hotels on Booking.com, twelve
  phones on Flipkart, or eight apartments on a rental app — and whichever
  one you land on last is the one you suddenly feel sure about, even if an
  earlier option was objectively just as good or better. A newly published
  study says that's not coincidence: the order you encounter options in
  measurably changes how committed you feel to your final pick, independent
  of the pick's actual merit.
- **Why it's underrated / why now:** This is a clean "why do WE" piece with
  a real, named mechanism — not a flat "comparison shopping is tiring"
  platitude. No comparison-shopping/search-behaviour piece has run in the
  logs checked (2026-09-12 through 2026-11-23), and the study is a genuine
  first-mover reframe: Temple University's own press write-up only went out
  in June 2026, and the finding — that commitment tracks *when* in a
  sequential search you found an option, not just how good it is — is
  distinct from the repetition-bias study used in the 2026-11-23 Topic 2
  (that one was about repeating a past choice; this is about where in a
  single search session an option sits).
- **Real anchor:** Hur, E., Woolley, K. & Tu, Y., "Converting preference to
  actual commitment: The role of option presentation order," *Journal of
  the Academy of Marketing Science* 54, 948–967 (2026); covered by Temple
  University's research office (10 June 2026). Across studies in digital
  and traditional retail (including an Airbnb-style rental search and an
  apartment search), consumers showed stronger commitment to the option
  they encountered *last* rather than earlier in a sequential search — a
  mechanism the authors call "perceived effort payoff" (the belief that the
  search effort was worthwhile). The effect weakens when all options are
  shown simultaneously instead of sequentially, or when the search is
  secondhand rather than firsthand — both boundary conditions the post
  would report accurately rather than overgeneralising to "any scrolling
  makes you decide badly."
- **Angle source:** General psychology/consumer-behaviour scan, standing in
  for the Raj Shamani / Vaibhav Sisinty framing lens this pass, since
  neither surfaced a fresh dated November post (see dropped list).
