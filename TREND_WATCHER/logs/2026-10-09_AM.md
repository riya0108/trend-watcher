## Full-debate-piece flags

### New this pass — full draft: The GST Council's arrest-powers removal

This topic has enough shape for a full debate piece: multiple confirmed
facts and named/attributed positions on both sides.

**Format:** Instagram carousel (a multi-layered policy-mechanics story suits
a carousel better than a Reel's single narrative arc).
**Story broke:** Oct 8, 2026, ~5:15-5:30pm IST.
**Publish-by deadline:** treat as urgent within the 24-48h window — this is
genuinely fresh and hasn't reached mainstream consumer coverage yet, so
there's real first-mover room before it saturates.

**Truth layer:**
- **FACT:** The 57th GST Council meeting took place Oct 8, 2026 (rescheduled
  from Oct 7), chaired by Finance Minister Nirmala Sitharaman.
- **FACT:** The Council recommended removing tax officials' power to arrest
  a person for most GST offences without a court order (a change to how
  Section 69 of the CGST Act is applied).
- **FACT:** The threshold for criminal prosecution under GST was raised
  five-fold, from ₹1 crore to ₹5 crore.
- **FACT:** The maximum general penalty under Section 125 of the CGST Act
  was reduced from ₹25,000 to ₹10,000; no notices will be issued for
  amounts below ₹10,000, and pending notices below that threshold will be
  withdrawn.
- **FACT:** A first phase of automated, risk-based refund processing was
  recommended: 90% of eligible refund claims for zero-rated supplies and
  inverted duty structures would be sanctioned provisionally without
  officer intervention.
- **FACT:** A simplified GST registration mechanism was recommended for
  small sellers on e-commerce platforms, letting them sell without
  establishing a place of business in every state they sell into.
- **ATTRIBUTED CLAIM:** FM Nirmala Sitharaman said that taking away arrest
  powers under GST "does not dilute action against offenders" (per
  reporting of her remarks).
- **ATTRIBUTED CLAIM:** PM Narendra Modi welcomed the reforms, citing
  faster decisions, lower compliance costs, automated refunds, and
  trust-based administration (per PIB/press reporting of his statement).
- **ATTRIBUTED CLAIM:** A Grant Thornton tax-controversy leader said the
  arrest power "served an important deterrent function, particularly
  against organised tax fraud" (per reporting).
- **ATTRIBUTED CLAIM:** Per reporting citing official enforcement data,
  central GST formations made 887 arrests between FY2021-22 and FY2024-25,
  not counting state-level enforcement figures, concentrated in fake-
  invoicing and fraudulent input-tax-credit (ITC) cases.
- **ATTRIBUTED CLAIM:** Reported business/industry voices argued the arrest
  provision was frequently "weaponised as harassment," particularly in
  sectors like banking and insurance, pressuring firms to pay disputed
  penalties rather than contest them through a drawn-out legal process.
- **ATTRIBUTED CLAIM:** Per reporting, Maharashtra and West Bengal's
  representatives had raised objections to GST officers holding arrest
  powers as far back as the Council's fifth meeting in December 2016, on
  ease-of-doing-business grounds.
- **INTERPRETATION:** The reform shifts most GST enforcement away from an
  immediate-arrest threat and toward civil recovery (tax, interest, and
  penalty) for the bulk of cases, reserving the possibility of prosecution
  for higher-value fraud above the new ₹5 crore threshold and requiring a
  court order to get there.
- **PREDICTION (not fact):** The changes require formal legislative
  amendment before taking effect; one report suggests implementation could
  land as late as "early 2027" — this is a forecast, not a confirmed date.
- **UNKNOWN:** The exact final legislative wording; how enforcement
  agencies plan to pursue fake-invoicing fraud without the arrest-threat
  lever specifically in the cases below the new ₹5 crore threshold; whether
  withdrawn notices below ₹10,000 will ever be revisited if new evidence
  emerges; how this interacts with the GST Council's separate, still-
  unresolved Section 16(2)/17(5) blocked-ITC proposals (deferred at this
  same meeting per one report).

**The debate:**
- **Side A (bull / for the reform):** This is overdue relief for honest
  traders. An arrest power with this much reach was always going to get
  misused as leverage — the reported pattern of forcing firms to settle
  disputed (not proven) penalties rather than litigate is a real cost to
  legitimate business, and no other Indian tax regime (VAT, service tax)
  ever needed arrest powers to function. A court-order requirement and a
  ₹5 crore threshold still leave room to go after serious fraud; this just
  stops low-value disputes from being treated like criminal cases.
- **Side B (bear / against the reform):** Fake invoicing and fraudulent ITC
  claims are not a fringe problem in India's GST system — they're the
  specific, named reason this power existed, and enforcement's own data
  (887 arrests in four years, almost entirely tied to this offence
  category) shows it was being used on exactly the target it was built
  for, not indiscriminately. Raising the prosecution bar 5x doesn't just
  remove harassment risk for small traders — it also raises the bar for
  mid-size, organised fraud operations to ever face anything beyond a
  civil recovery notice, at a point when fake-billing rackets have been a
  recurring, scaled enforcement problem.
- **Why both sides are real:** Neither side disputes the underlying
  numbers — both the "ease of doing business" case and the "deterrence"
  case cite the same enforcement history (887 arrests, mostly fraud-linked)
  and draw opposite conclusions about whether that history justifies
  keeping or removing the power.

**So-what:** For the overwhelming majority of GST-registered businesses who
have never committed fraud, this is a direct reduction in legal exposure
and harassment risk. Whether it also quietly raises the ceiling for how
much fake-invoice fraud can run before it's treated as a criminal matter —
rather than a bill the business can settle and walk away from — is the
open question that determines who actually benefits.

**Structure sketch:**
1. Hook: "The taxman just gave up the power to arrest you over GST — what's
   the catch?"
2. Facts as established: what changed (arrest powers, threshold, penalty,
   automation), when, and who announced it.
3. Side A, stated fairly: relief for small/legitimate business.
4. Side B, stated fairly: risk to fraud deterrence, with the 887-arrest
   enforcement data point.
5. So-what: who actually benefits, and what to watch for (the legislative
   text, and how the government handles the organised-fraud threshold).
6. CTA — invite the audience's own take, don't tell them the answer.
