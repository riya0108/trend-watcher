### 2. RBI's new cross-border card rule: safer payments, or more declined transactions?

Format: carousel (suits "what changed → why → the industry's own warning →
what to do if it happens to you").
Story broke: compliance deadline Oct 1, 2026.
Publish-by deadline: within 24-48h of this pass, while this remains ahead
of mainstream consumer coverage.

**Truth layer:**
- FACT: Effective Oct 1, 2026, RBI requires Indian card issuers to apply
  additional authentication to non-recurring, cross-border,
  card-not-present transactions when requested by the overseas merchant or
  acquirer, plus a risk-based authentication mechanism for all such
  transactions, and to register their Bank Identification Numbers with
  international card networks. This follows RBI's broader domestic 2FA
  mandate that took effect April 1, 2026. If an issuer fails to comply and
  a customer suffers a loss as a result, the issuer must compensate the
  customer in full.
- ATTRIBUTED CLAIM: Payments-industry commentary (per sources including
  ecorpit.com's compliance guidance) has flagged a risk that cross-border
  e-commerce transactions could be declined, or abandoned by customers
  mid-purchase, where issuers or overseas merchants/networks are not fully
  ready to support the new authentication flow.
- INTERPRETATION: The compensation requirement shifts meaningful risk onto
  issuing banks for getting this right, which is a reading of the rule's
  likely effect on bank behavior, not a stated RBI objective.
- OPINION: Not pre-decided by this pass — the human writer should choose
  and label the editorial stance deliberately.
- PREDICTION: Whether failed/declined cross-border transactions actually
  materialize at scale, or whether issuers and global networks adapt
  smoothly, is unresolved and speculative.
- UNKNOWN: How many Indian card issuers and international merchants were
  fully compliant by the Oct 1 deadline; actual decline-rate data for
  cross-border transactions since the deadline (not available this pass).

**The debate:**
- Side A (this is a clear consumer win): closing the cross-border
  authentication gap brings international card payments up to the same
  security standard as domestic ones, cuts fraud exposure, and puts the
  compensation burden on banks rather than consumers when something goes
  wrong.
- Side B (good intentions, real friction risk): a hard compliance deadline
  across banks, international card networks, and foreign merchants
  simultaneously is a lot of moving parts to get right at once, and the
  payments industry's own stated concern is that genuine transactions —
  tuition payments, emergency travel bookings — could fail exactly when
  they matter most.
- Why both sides are real: the security/consumer-protection rationale is
  written into the rule itself (including the compensation clause); the
  friction risk is a concern raised by the payments industry actually
  implementing it, not a hypothetical objection.

**So-what:** anyone who pays a foreign website or app with an Indian card
— for subscriptions, travel, or tuition — is directly affected by how
smoothly this rollout goes.

1. Hook: "A new RBI rule just changed how you pay foreign websites."
2. Facts as established (what the rule requires, the compensation clause)
3. Side A stated fairly (security and consumer protection)
